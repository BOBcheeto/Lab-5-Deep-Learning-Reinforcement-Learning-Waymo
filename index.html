Over six structured steps, I worked through a simulated Waymo strategy analyst assignment — from the basics of how neural networks learn, to designing a reward function for an autonomous planner, to writing a city-launch recommendation memo for a VP of Strategy. The Situation

Two weeks ago in Austin, Texas, a Waymo car hesitated for 14 seconds at an unprotected left turn. No safety issue — but the rider gave it a 2-star review and tweeted. Engineering flagged it as both a perception problem and a planner problem. The VP of Strategy asked me to figure out what that meant, and to recommend which city Waymo should launch in next.

1

Foundation

Deep Learning Basics

I started by training a small neural network from scratch — labeling images as "cat" or "not cat" and watching how the network's internal feature weights shifted with each new labeled example. The exercise made the abstract mechanics concrete: a neural network isn't programmed with rules, it infers them from data, and the quality of its output is bounded entirely by the quality of what it was trained on.

I then studied a four-stage visualization of a convolutional neural network processing a pedestrian image — raw pixels becoming edge detectors, edges becoming body-part recognizers, and body parts becoming a final classification. The final concept was the most strategically important: the long-tail problem. A handful of common scenarios dominate real-world miles, but it's the thousands of rare scenarios — costumed children, wheelchair users, murals painted on truck sides — that together account for most AI failures. A model trained on common cases will be overconfident precisely where it needs to be most careful.

Three concepts I carried forward: neural networks learn from labeled examples and cannot generalize beyond their training distribution; CNNs build understanding layer by layer, which makes the early data-quality decisions irreversible; and the long-tail problem means a model is only as good as the edge cases in its training data.

2

Perception

Deep Learning at Waymo

I played the role of a Waymo ML analyst evaluating three training-dataset vendors: Dataset A (sunny Phoenix frames only, $100M), Dataset B (balanced conditions, $180M), and Dataset C (edge-case weighted, $300M). I trained a simulated perception network on each and observed how accuracy diverged on routine versus rare test scenes — the gap between Dataset A and Dataset C performance was largest exactly where it mattered most.

My recommendation was Dataset C. The $120M premium over Dataset B is real — but the alternative is a model that performs well in daylight Phoenix and fails in the conditions that actually produce serious incidents. I drafted the following email to Waymo's CFO making that case.

Artifact — CFO Recommendation Email

Hi [CFO Name],

Following my vendor evaluation, I'm recommending Dataset C ($300M) — the edge-case-weighted dataset — over the two lower-cost alternatives.

Three reasons:

First, safety record exposure. AVs fail publicly on rare scenarios — adverse weather, unusual pedestrian behavior, ambiguous intersections. Dataset A's Phoenix daylight bias leaves these gaps unaddressed, which is where incidents happen.

Second, regulatory and legal risk. Regulators and plaintiffs don't grade on a curve for "rare conditions." A single attributable failure from undertrained edge cases could dwarf the $200M price difference in liability and remediation costs.

Third, competitive positioning. Our safety leadership is a core differentiator. Compromising training data quality to save cost is the kind of decision that surfaces in post-incident discovery.

The tradeoff I'm explicitly accepting: a $120M premium over Dataset B. Given the risk profile, I believe that's the right call.

Happy to walk through the full analysis.

3

Simulation

World Models

Dataset C addresses the training data problem — but it can't fully solve the long tail, because some edge cases are too rare to collect even with aggressive data purchasing. That's where the world model comes in. I explored how Waymo uses a simulation environment trained on reality to generate scenarios that have never been recorded: composing weather, time of day, actor types, and wildcards into novel combinations on demand.

The strategic implication I found most interesting was the contrast with Tesla. Tesla's fleet passively accumulates miles at enormous scale — no one matches their volume. But volume can't be targeted. Waymo's world model can identify exactly where the current model is weakest, generate hundreds of variations of that specific scenario, and retrain overnight. These are orthogonal advantages, not substitutes. I drafted the following brief arguing that world model investment is a strategic moat, not just an R&D line item.

Artifact — Internal Investment Brief

To: [VP Name]  |  From: [Your Name]  |  Re: Simulation Investment Rationale

The world model's core advantage over fleet driving is the ability to request reality rather than wait for it.

Fleet driving accumulates miles broadly. The world model generates scenarios surgically. To encounter a single edge case — say, a wheelchair user partially concealed behind a stopped truck, at night, in snow — organically would require roughly 54 miles of real-world driving with no guarantee of that specific combination. Our world model constructed that scenario in seconds by composing conditions it had learned separately. It can then generate hundreds of variations and retrain overnight.

On Tesla: Their volume advantage is real — no one can match their total miles. But volume optimizes for the common case. The long tail of rare, dangerous scenarios isn't solved by more of the same driving. Waymo's targeting advantage — the ability to identify exactly where our model is weakest and practice that scenario repeatedly — addresses a fundamentally different problem. Volume and targeting aren't substitutes; they're orthogonal strengths.

If we stop investing in simulation: We retain our current safety envelope but lose the ability to close new gaps faster than edge cases find us in the real world. Tesla improves by accumulating miles. We improve by designing our own tests. Defunding simulation means trading a proactive safety posture for a reactive one — and ceding the mechanism by which we currently outpace the long tail.

4

Planning

Reinforcement Learning & the Reward Function

The Austin hesitation wasn't just a perception failure — it was a planner failure. The car saw the gap correctly and still didn't go. That's a reward function problem: the planner had been trained to weight collision avoidance so heavily that it became overly conservative at ambiguous intersections. I was given a simulator with a fixed 20-point penalty budget and four sliders — one reward (turn completion: +10) and three penalties (collision: up to -10, close call: up to -5, wait time: up to -5) — and asked to find a configuration that cleared three defensibility bars: ≥285 completions, ≤1 collision, and average wait under 8 seconds across 300 test attempts.

The central tension was that reducing the wait penalty to encourage faster decisions also increased close-call frequency. I resolved it by weighting collision most heavily at -10, accepting a slightly longer average wait in exchange for a zero-collision result. The policy cleared all three bars: 292 completions, 0 collisions, 5.5-second average wait. I drafted the following paragraph to defend that configuration to Waymo's safety board.

Artifact — Safety Board Defense

The reward function was designed around three measurable bars: a completion rate of at least 285 successful turns out of 300, a collision count of no more than one, and an average wait time under eight seconds. The trained policy met all three — 292 completions, zero collisions, and a 5.5-second average wait. The central design tension was between hesitation and risk: reducing the wait penalty aggressively improved completion rates but also increased close-call frequency, indicating the policy was accepting tighter margins to act faster. To resolve that tension, collision was weighted most heavily in the penalty budget at -10, with close calls at -5 and wait time at -5, reflecting a deliberate priority ordering where physical safety supersedes operational efficiency. The consequence of that choice is a wait time that, at 5.5 seconds, is longer than strictly necessary to clear the completion threshold — the policy is more conservative than the minimum passing bar requires. That conservatism was an explicit design decision, not a calibration failure, and it is reflected in the zero-collision result.

Business Strategy

Data Strategy & the AI Business

Stepping back from the technical work, I studied the business logic underneath Waymo's strategy: why the world model investment produces a durable moat, how Waymo's simulation-first approach differs from Tesla's fleet-first one, and what makes an AI company's data advantage hard to replicate. I learned three diagnostic questions — where does the data come from, does the pipeline compound, and can a competitor replicate it with money — and then allocated a hypothetical $1B Waymo data budget across fleet, simulation, labelers, sensors, and partnerships to see the tradeoffs live.

To test whether I'd internalized the framework, I applied the same three questions to a different AI company. I chose Spotify — a scaled AI business in a completely different domain — and the analysis is below.

Artifact — Data Moat Evaluation: Spotify

Where does the data come from?

Spotify's core dataset is behavioral: 675+ million users generating continuous signals — streams, skips, saves, playlist adds, repeat listens, session duration, and shuffle patterns. Crucially, it's not just what people play but how they play it — a skip at 0:18 versus 1:45 carries different signal. They also pull in podcast consumption patterns, search queries, and Canvas video engagement. The Wrapped product is essentially a consumer-facing proof-of-concept for how granularly they can segment listening behavior. Underneath all of this sits their audio analysis layer — acoustic features like tempo, key, energy, and danceability extracted from every track in their catalog — which feeds their recommendation engine alongside the behavioral data.

Does the pipeline compound?

Yes, and this is where Spotify's position is genuinely strong. Every new user improves collaborative filtering by adding more nodes to the taste graph. Every new track gets contextualized faster because the behavioral baseline is deeper. Daylist and the DJ feature are structural evidence of this: they require not just historical data but real-time responsiveness to micro-session behavior, which only works at scale. The pipeline also compounds across content types — behavioral patterns from music inform podcast recommendations and vice versa — creating cross-domain flywheel effects that weren't present five years ago.

Can a competitor replicate it with money?

Not quickly, and not fully. Apple Music can buy catalog access and hire recommendation engineers, but they can't buy 10 years of skip patterns from 675 million people across 180 markets. The behavioral history is time-locked — it accumulated through incumbency, not capital. The closest threat is YouTube Music, which has its own scaled behavioral graph from video engagement, and could plausibly build a comparable taste model over time. But Spotify's podcast and audiobook consumption data — acquired through Anchor, Megaphone, and Findaway — adds a multimodal layer that's harder to replicate piecemeal.

Verdict: Medium-strong moat — the behavioral graph is genuinely difficult to replicate and actively compounds, but it's not insurmountable for a well-resourced platform incumbent (Google, Apple) that already operates at comparable user scale in adjacent contexts.

Final Deliverable

The City Launch Recommendation

The final step was to bring everything together: the dataset choice, the world model investment rationale, the reward function design, the data budget allocation, and the social-license analysis. I studied three real-world Waymo social-license incidents — protest "coning" in San Francisco, a blocked fire truck in Austin, taxi-union opposition in Miami — and weighed four candidate cities (Miami, Seattle, Atlanta, New York) against each dimension of the analysis.

My recommendation was Seattle. The weather edge cases it produces align directly with the Dataset C investment, the cautious planner profile fits the driving environment, and the stakeholder opposition — while organized — is addressable through concrete commitments rather than broad public persuasion campaigns. The memo below is what I presented to the VP of Strategy.

Artifact — VP Strategy Recommendation Memo

To: VP of Strategy  |  From: Ryder Chase

Re: City #5 Launch Recommendation — Seattle  |  Date: May 2, 2026

Recommendation

Launch City #5 in Seattle. The combination of a safety-conscious driving culture, a high-value data environment, and an addressable stakeholder landscape makes it the strongest risk-adjusted choice — and the window to establish first-mover positioning before a competitor enters the Pacific Northwest is closing.

Three Reasons Seattle is the Right City

1. Tech Readiness. Seattle's defining edge cases — constant rain, steep hills, and black ice — are exactly the conditions our perception model needs to close. We've already committed to Dataset C, the edge-case-weighted training set, which directly addresses low-visibility and adverse-surface scenarios. Seattle will stress-test that investment immediately and generate real-world validation data that no simulation can fully substitute. Our reward function produced a cautious driving profile, which is well-suited to a city where road conditions change fast and margins for error are narrower than in our existing markets.

2. Data Moat Growth. Every mile logged in Seattle compounds into a weather edge-case library that competitors cannot buy or shortcut. Rain + hills + black ice is a combination that Phoenix, Austin, and San Francisco don't produce at scale. Our $1B budget reflects this priority: 30% to simulation (to pre-generate black ice and steep-grade scenarios before launch) and 20% each to fleet deployment, labelers, and sensors — with 10% reserved for city and transit partnerships. The data moat Seattle grows is narrow but deep, and it's the moat that matters most for expanding into other northern and coastal markets.

3. Social License Path. Seattle's risk isn't public skepticism — it's organized opposition from specific, legitimate stakeholders: ATU transit workers and disability and bike-access advocates. Both groups have concrete interests, not ideological opposition to AVs. The playbook is engagement before launch, not after. First engagement priority: ATU leadership, with a commitment that Waymo will not seek to displace existing transit routes in the first 24 months and will share safety data quarterly. Second: disability and bike-access coalitions, with an ADA-forward rider experience commitment and a dedicated feedback channel built into the app. On the broader safety narrative — Waymo's own data shows a roughly 3.5× improvement in injury-producing crashes versus human drivers, with a greater than 90% reduction in the most serious categories. The honest frame for skeptics isn't "is Waymo safe?" — it's "compared to human drivers, who kill ~38,000 Americans annually." That reframe belongs in every stakeholder conversation, but it supplements community commitments; it doesn't replace them.

The Risk I'm Accepting

Seattle's weather will generate operational disruptions in year one — sensor degradation in heavy rain, edge cases our simulation didn't fully anticipate on steep grades. I'm accepting a higher incident-investigation rate than our current markets in exchange for the data and positioning upside. Mitigation: deploy a dedicated Seattle safety response team at launch, establish a 48-hour public incident reporting commitment, and pre-brief the city's transportation department on the review protocol before the first vehicle goes live.

What I'd Watch in the First 6 Months

Three signals will tell us whether this was the right call: (1) disengagement rate on steep-grade and wet-road scenarios — if it tracks above our Phoenix baseline by more than 15%, our simulation investment needs reallocation; (2) ATU and advocate sentiment at the 90-day stakeholder review — if either group has escalated publicly, the community relations plan needs acceleration, not defense; and (3) Dataset C real-world validation rate — are the edge cases Seattle is producing matching what we trained for, or are we encountering scenario categories we didn't anticipate? The answer to that third question informs not just Seattle's trajectory, but the world model investment case for every city after it.

What I Learned

AI business concepts I can now apply to any company — not just Waymo.

  • The long-tail problem: AI models fail at rare scenarios, not common ones. A model's real-world ceiling is set by the edge cases in its training data, not its average-case performance.
  • World models and transfer learning: The most defensible AI systems don't just collect data — they generate it. A world model trained on general reality can compose novel scenarios from learned ingredients, turning simulation into a strategic weapon against the long tail.
  • The alignment problem: You can't specify every goal in a reward function, so the tradeoffs you encode — implicitly or explicitly — become the system's values. Reward function design is strategy, not just engineering.
  • The data moat: The best AI data advantages are time-locked. Behavioral history, rare scenario libraries, and compounding pipelines accumulate through incumbency — they can't be purchased or replicated quickly by a well-funded competitor entering later.
  • Compounding advantage: Data pipelines that improve automatically with scale are structurally different from one-shot datasets. The former creates a widening gap; the latter creates a one-time lead that erodes.
  • Social license as a launch variable: Technical readiness is necessary but not sufficient for AV deployment. The stakeholder landscape — unions, disability advocates, regulators — determines whether a technically sound launch succeeds or stalls. Engagement before launch is cheaper than recovery after opposition organizes.
